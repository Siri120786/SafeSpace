# PCOS and PCOD: research notes behind the Cycle Care hub

These notes explain why the PCOS / PCOD hub treats the two labels the way it does. They come from a quick web search (Oct 2026) and are **not clinical review**. A doctor or dietitian must review the wording before real users see it (see `content-needing-review.md`).

## 1. What the sources say about the two labels

- **Medical terminology.** The standard medical term is **PCOS** (polycystic ovary syndrome). The Rotterdam criteria (2003), still the most widely used, define PCOS as meeting two of three: irregular or absent ovulation, signs of androgen excess, and polycystic-looking ovaries on ultrasound. The 2023 international evidence-based guideline (Teede et al., *J Clin Endocrinol Metab* 108(10), 2447-2469) also uses PCOS only.
- **"PCOD".** *Polycystic ovarian disease* is an older or looser label, common in India and South Asia. Many clinicians treat it as the same condition as PCOS. Many Indian clinic and hospital pages describe it as the **milder, more lifestyle-responsive picture** (ovaries releasing many immature eggs, irregular periods, fewer metabolic effects), and describe PCOS as the **broader hormonal and metabolic condition** with higher long-term risks (type 2 diabetes, heart disease) and more often needing medicine.
- **So the distinction is popular, not formal.** The clinic blogs are marketing-style sources and disagree in places. The app therefore never decides which one a person has. The person picks the label their own doctor uses ("PCOS", "PCOD" or "Not sure yet"), and the hub always says to ask the doctor which applies.

## 2. How the app handles each label (same features for both)

Every feature exists for PCOS, PCOD and "Not sure". Only the emphasis of the wording changes.

| Area | PCOS emphasis | PCOD emphasis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| About | Hormonal syndrome, two-of-three diagnosis, insulin resistance, long-term check-ups | Popular label, milder and often lifestyle-responsive, still worth a doctor visit |
| Doctor-visit questions | Blood sugar, cholesterol and blood pressure checks, insulin resistance, hormone tests, fertility planning | Period regularity, whether tests are needed, how often to follow up, painful or heavy periods |
| Food tips | Steady meals with fibre and protein, slower-digesting carbohydrates | Regular balanced meals, enough fibre, less refined sugar |
| Movement tips | About 150 minutes moderate activity a week plus two days of muscle-strengthening (general reference) | Regular moderate activity, walking, light strength and yoga |
| Myths | PCOS-only myths (same symptoms, only periods and fertility, "gone" when periods regulate) | PCOD-only myths (cannot be managed, crash diets fix it, irregular periods are normal so skip the doctor) |
| Shared | Cysts must be removed, only overweight people, never a baby, pills cure it, your fault, "the same or totally different" | Same |

## 3. Exercise evidence used

- A 2024 exercise position statement building on the 2023 guideline: adults with PCOS should aim for **150-300 minutes of moderate** or **75-150 minutes of vigorous** aerobic activity a week, plus **two days of muscle-strengthening** (the strength part comes from a secondary source, not the guideline text).
- **No single exercise type has been shown to be best.**
- **Yoga:** the evidence is thin. One small randomised trial (Patel et al., 2020, *J Am Osteopath Assoc*, 31 women) used 1-hour mindful yoga classes three times a week (about 180 minutes) and saw lower free testosterone and less depression and anxiety, but only 22 finished. The app presents yoga as one helpful habit, not a treatment.
- The app shows ~150 minutes only as **general information**. Any weekly goal is optional and set by the person.

## 4. Safeguards built in (and why)

PCOS is linked with a higher risk of eating disorders, and over-exercise is a risk too. So:

- The person writes their own diet plan. The app does not generate diets, count calories, set a target weight, calculate BMI or interpret blood values.
- Rewards are the team's choice (stars and streaks), with a **switch to hide them**. Partial days still count, rest days are part of the weekly plan, and a very long exercise day gets a "rest matters" note.
- The character cheers progress and nudges kindly. It never shames and never mentions the body. Tests check every line.
- Crash diets and skipping meals are called out as not recommended.
- All new data (diet plan, rewards, movement, health profile, chosen label) stays **on the device**, with a "Clear all my PCOS / PCOD data" button.
